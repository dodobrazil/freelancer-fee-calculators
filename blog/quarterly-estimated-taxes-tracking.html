How to Track Quarterly Estimated Taxes as a Freelancer (A Simple System)

US-focused Examples and links refer to US rules.

When you work for yourself, nobody withholds tax from your payments. That makes you the payroll department, and "quarterly estimated taxes" becomes part of your routine. The good news: you do not need accounting software to stay on top of it. You need a simple system and a monthly habit.

This guide is general information about record-keeping, not tax advice. Rules differ by country and, in the US, by your situation. Check the official sources linked below or ask a tax professional.

Why tracking matters

In the US, people who expect to owe tax beyond what is withheld are generally expected to pay during the year rather than all at once at filing time. The IRS explains who this applies to and how to pay on its estimated taxes page: https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/estimated-taxes. Paying late or too little can lead to penalties, so the safest habit is to know your numbers throughout the year.

Tracking also protects your cash flow. If tax money sits in the same account as your spending money, it is easy to treat it as yours. A system that separates the two removes that temptation.

Where to find the real rules

Do not rely on a blog post, including this one, for rates, thresholds or due dates. They change. Use these instead:

  • IRS Form 1040-ES and its instructions, which include a worksheet for estimating payments: https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-1040-es
  • IRS Publication 505, Tax Withholding and Estimated Tax: https://www.irs.gov/publications/p505
  • The IRS page on self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare): https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employment-tax-social-security-and-medicare-taxes

If you live outside the US, your own tax authority has equivalent pages. The tracking system below works the same way anywhere.

Pick a set-aside percentage

A set-aside percentage is the share of each payment you move to a separate savings account for taxes. It is a planning shortcut, not a tax calculation.

To choose one, work through the 1040-ES worksheet (or your country's equivalent) using a realistic estimate of this year's profit. Compare the total tax it suggests with your expected income to get a rough percentage. Or ask an accountant for a starting figure. Write the percentage in one cell of your sheet so you can change it in one place.

Revisit it a couple of times a year. If a big client arrives or work dries up, the percentage that fit in January may not fit in September.

Log every payment as gross, fee and net

Record three numbers for each payment:

  • Gross: what the client paid or what the invoice said.
  • Platform or processing fee: what the marketplace or payment service kept.
  • Net: gross minus fee, which is what reached you.

Why bother? Your records should match what your bank shows and what your platform reports, so the year-end totals reconcile. Whether fees are deductible, and whether income is reported gross or net, depends on your situation, so ask a professional rather than assuming. Keeping all three numbers lets you answer either way.

A simple sheet needs these columns: date, client, gross, fee, net, note. In Google Sheets the net formula is =IF(C2="","",C2-D2).

A monthly 20-minute checklist

  1. Collect payments. Compare your sheet with bank and platform statements and add anything missing.
  2. Total the month. Gross, fees and net.
  3. Move the set-aside. Apply your percentage to the base your adviser recommends (net or gross) and transfer it to the tax savings account.
  4. Log expenses. Software, equipment, coworking and similar costs. Keep receipts. Which ones are deductible is a question for IRS guidance or your adviser.
  5. Check the calendar. Look up the next estimated payment date on the IRS site, not from memory, and confirm the savings account will cover it.
  6. Note surprises. A large new contract or a lost client? Flag it so you can revisit your percentage.

Each quarter: make the payment

When a payment date arrives, take the amount from your 1040-ES worksheet or your adviser's figure and pay through a channel the IRS lists. Keep the confirmation. Then record the payment in your sheet so your year-to-date "paid" total matches your records.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing money. Running income through the account you spend from makes everything harder. A separate savings account for tax is the cheapest fix.
  • Never updating the percentage. Income moves, so your estimate should too.
  • Reconstructing from memory. Log payments weekly or monthly, not at quarter end.
  • Ignoring fees. Platform fees quietly reduce your income. Record them as they happen.
  • Treating a blog as a tax authority. Verify every rule on the official page.

Putting it together

The system is small: one percentage, one income log, one monthly checklist and one savings account. Once it is in place, quarterly deadlines become routine rather than a scramble.
