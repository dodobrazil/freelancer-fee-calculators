Safe Harbor Rules for Freelancers: How Much Estimated Tax Is "Enough"?

US-focused Examples and links refer to US rules.

If you freelance in the US, one question comes up every quarter: how much do I actually have to send the IRS to avoid a penalty? You do not have to predict your income perfectly. The IRS offers "safe harbor" rules: if you pay at least a certain amount during the year, you generally avoid the underpayment penalty, even if your final bill turns out higher.

This article explains the rules in plain English and shows a worked example. It is general information, not tax advice.

First: do you need to pay estimated tax at all?

Generally, you must pay estimated tax if you expect to owe at least $1,000 for the year after subtracting withholding and refundable credits. If you expect to owe less than that, estimated payments are not required. Sources: IRS Publication 505 and IRS Topic 306.

The two safe harbors

You generally avoid the penalty if your withholding and estimated payments add up to at least the smaller of:

  1. 90% of this year's tax, or
  2. 100% of the tax shown on last year's return.

If your adjusted gross income (AGI) last year was above $150,000 ($75,000 if married filing separately), the second option rises to 110% of last year's tax. Source: Publication 505, "Higher Income Taxpayers."

Why this matters for freelancers: your income is uneven and hard to forecast. Option 2 lets you pick a number early in the year, based on a return you have already filed, and stick to it.

Worked example

Imagine last year's total tax was $9,000 and your AGI was under $150,000. This year you expect your tax to be about $12,000.

  • 90% of this year's expected tax: $10,800
  • 100% of last year's tax: $9,000
  • The smaller number is $9,000, or $2,250 per quarter.

If you earn more than expected, you may still owe the difference when you file, but under these rules you generally would not owe the underpayment penalty. (The numbers are illustrative only; your own situation can differ.)

When income is lumpy: the annualized method

If you earn most of your money late in the year, equal quarterly payments may overshoot early on. The IRS lets you "annualize" income and make unequal payments, and you check it on Form 2210. It takes more work, so keep clean records of income by quarter. See IRS Topic 306.

Due dates

Estimated payments are generally due April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15 of the following year. If a date falls on a weekend or legal holiday, the next business day counts. The IRS also notes that if you file your return by January 31 and pay the remaining tax, you can skip the January payment. Check the exact dates for your tax year in Publication 505 or Form 1040-ES: about Form 1040-ES.

A simple routine

  1. Find last year's total tax on your filed return.
  2. Decide which safe harbor fits you (and whether the 110% rule applies).
  3. Divide by four for a baseline payment.
  4. Log each real payment and your running income, so you can adjust if the year goes very differently.
