Self-Employment Tax Explained: How Much of Each Freelance Payment to Set Aside

US-focused Examples and links refer to US rules.

Employees see Social Security and Medicare tax come out of every paycheck, with the employer paying a matching half. When you work for yourself, you pay both halves. That is self-employment tax, and it surprises many first-year freelancers because it sits on top of regular income tax.

This is general information, not tax advice.

The basic numbers

According to the IRS (self-employment tax, Topic 554):

  • The rate is 15.3%: 12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare.
  • It applies to 92.35% of your net earnings from self-employment (your profit, not your revenue).
  • You generally owe it if net earnings are $400 or more.
  • You can deduct one-half of the self-employment tax when you figure your adjusted gross income.
  • It is calculated on Schedule SE.

The Social Security part (12.4%) applies only up to an annual wage base that changes each year. Check the current limit on the IRS page about self-employment tax. If you also have a W-2 job, your wages count toward that limit.

Worked example

Say your freelance profit for the year is $60,000.

  • 92.35% of $60,000 = $55,410
  • 15.3% of $55,410 = about $8,478 of self-employment tax
  • Half of that, about $4,239, is deductible when figuring income tax.

So before you pay any income tax, roughly 14% of profit ($8,478 divided by $60,000) goes to self-employment tax. Income tax is separate and depends on your total situation.

Turning this into a set-aside habit

A fixed percentage is easier to live with than a calculation on every invoice. A common approach:

  1. Estimate your yearly profit (revenue minus business expenses).
  2. Estimate self-employment tax with the steps above.
  3. Add a rough income tax estimate based on your own average rate (a tax professional or the Form 1040-ES worksheet can help).
  4. Divide the total by your expected revenue. That is your set-aside percentage.
  5. Move that share of every payment into a separate savings account the day it arrives.

Revisit the percentage each quarter. If profit rises or falls, your rate changes.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Using revenue instead of profit. Self-employment tax is based on net earnings, so track expenses.
  • Forgetting that W-2 wages count toward the Social Security limit.
  • Skipping quarterly payments. Self-employment tax is part of what estimated payments must cover. See the IRS estimated taxes page.
