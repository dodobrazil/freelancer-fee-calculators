The formula, and why not just add the percentage

PayPal takes its percentage from the whole payment, including the fee part. So the invoice must satisfy invoice x (1 - rate) - fixed = target, which gives invoice = (target + fixed) / (1 - rate). For $500 net at 4.99%: (500 + 0.49) / 0.9501 = $526.78. The naive $500 x 1.0499 + $0.49 = $525.44 is about $1.34 short.

FAQ

How do I calculate the PayPal fee to add to an invoice?

Divide, do not multiply. To net a target, invoice = (target + fixed fee) / (1 - rate). For $500 with the 3.49% rate plus the $0.49 fixed fee, that is $518.59. Adding 3.49% and the fixed fee to $500 gives $517.94, which leaves you slightly short because PayPal takes its percentage from the larger total.

Why does simply adding the percentage leave me short?

PayPal charges its percentage on the whole payment, including the part that covers the fee. On an international $500 net target, adding 4.99% + $0.49 gives $525.44, but PayPal would take $26.71 and leave $498.73. The correct invoice is $526.78.

Am I allowed to pass the PayPal fee on to my client?

We could not confirm PayPal's current rules on surcharging from an official page, and card network rules and local law vary by country. Many freelancers instead build the fee into their quoted price, which avoids a separate line item. Check PayPal's user agreement and your local rules before adding a fee line.

Does the international 1.5% apply to every client?

Only when the payer is in another country, per PayPal's US page. For a mixed client base, quote one price using the international rate or use the domestic column for local clients.

What about currency conversion?

If PayPal converts the payment, a 3% or 4% spread comes on top. The PayPal fee calculator has a conversion option that includes it when you solve for the invoice amount.
